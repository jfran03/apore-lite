# Atomic Mass, Atomic Weight, and the Mole

## Definition
The quantities used to describe the mass bookkeeping of an atom are the atomic number (Z), the atomic mass (A), isotopes, atomic weight, and the amu (equivalently, 1 gram/mole).
> Source: F26-D2L-Chapter 2- Introduction to Engineering Materials-Part 1 and part 2 (full).pdf, slide 4

## Key Concepts
- **Approximate particle masses:** proton ≈ 1 amu, neutron ≈ 1 amu, electron ≈ 0.00055 amu. 1 amu ≈ 1.67 × 10⁻²⁴ g, or 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.
  > Source: F26-D2L-Chapter 2- Introduction to Engineering Materials-Part 1 and part 2 (full).pdf, slide 4
- **Adding up an atom's mass:** one carbon (isotope C-12) atom contains 6 protons + 6 neutrons, so its approximate mass is 6(1 amu) + 6(1 amu) = 12 amu. One helium atom contains approximately two protons and two neutrons: 2(1 amu) + 2(1 amu) = 4 amu.
  > Source: F26-D2L-Chapter 2- Introduction to Engineering Materials-Part 1 and part 2 (full).pdf, slide 4
- **Why atoms need their own mass unit:** grams are convenient in the laboratory but far too large for describing one atom — one carbon-12 atom weighs only ≈ 1.99 × 10⁻²³ g. Scientists agreed to use carbon-12 as the reference: 1 amu = 1/12 of the mass of one carbon-12 atom, so one carbon-12 atom = 12 amu. The amu is also written u (unified atomic mass unit). Atomic mass in amu lets us write a simple number instead of an extremely tiny mass in grams.
  > Source: F26-D2L-Chapter 2- Introduction to Engineering Materials-Part 1 and part 2 (full).pdf, slide 9
- **The mole as a counting unit:** a dozen and a mole are both counting units — the idea is the same, but the size of the counting group is very different. A dozen = 12 objects; a mole = 6.022 × 10²³ particles. 12 g of carbon-12 = 1 mol of carbon-12 = 6.022 × 10²³ (Avogadro's number) carbon atoms.
  > Source: F26-D2L-Chapter 2- Introduction to Engineering Materials-Part 1 and part 2 (full).pdf, slide 5
- **Numerical bridge from amu to grams:** one mole of carbon-12 is 12 g containing 6.022 × 10²³ atoms, and each carbon-12 atom has a mass of 12 amu. Dividing both relationships by 12 gives 1 amu = 1 g ÷ (6.022 × 10²³) = 0.166 × 10⁻²³ g ≈ 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g. The bridge is: 12 amu per atom ↔ 12 g per mole.
  > Source: F26-D2L-Chapter 2- Introduction to Engineering Materials-Part 1 and part 2 (full).pdf, slide 8
- **Mass versus weight:** mass stays the same; weight changes with gravity. W = m × g (weight = mass × gravitational acceleration). Mass is the amount of matter, unit kilogram (kg); weight is the force exerted by gravity, unit newton (N). A 60 kg person has the same mass on Earth and the Moon, but on Earth W = (60 kg)(9.81 m/s²) = 588.6 N ≈ 589 N, while on the Moon W = (60 kg)(1.62 m/s²) = 97.2 N ≈ 97 N.
  > Source: F26-D2L-Chapter 2- Introduction to Engineering Materials-Part 1 and part 2 (full).pdf, slide 6
- **Isotopes:** atoms of the same element having the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons. The mass number is the number of protons + number of neutrons; the atomic number is the number of protons. For carbon: Carbon-12 (6 protons, 6 neutrons, natural abundance 98.93%), Carbon-13 (6 protons, 7 neutrons, natural abundance 1.07%), Carbon-14 (6 protons, 8 neutrons, natural abundance trace amount).
  > Source: F26-D2L-Chapter 2- Introduction to Engineering Materials-Part 1 and part 2 (full).pdf, slide 12
- **Atomic mass versus atomic weight:** atomic mass is the mass of an individual atom, whereas atomic weight is the average (weighted) of the atomic masses of an atom's naturally occurring isotopes.
  > Source: F26-D2L-Chapter 2- Introduction to Engineering Materials-Part 1 and part 2 (full).pdf, slide 13
- **Atomic weight formula:** atomic weight = Σ(isotope mass)(fractional abundance).
  > Source: F26-D2L-Chapter 2- Introduction to Engineering Materials-Part 1 and part 2 (full).pdf, slide 14
- **Mole is a number unit:** a mole is like kilo (10³) or mega (10⁶) — a number unit, so it does not carry a physical unit like gram or newton. One mole has 6.022 × 10²³ particles, which may be a group of atoms or a group of molecules.
  > Source: EXAMPLE PROBLEMS 1 - WEEK 2.docx, Problem 4

## Examples
- **Example Problem 1 — atomic weight of natural uranium:** natural uranium has atomic number 92 and consists of three main isotopes: 99.274% of ²³⁸U, 0.720% of ²³⁵U, and 0.006% of ²³⁴U. Using the isotope mass numbers as approximate masses, A_U = (238)(0.99274) + (235)(0.00720) + (234)(0.00006) = 236.27212 + 1.69200 + 0.01404 = 237.97816 amu.
  > Source: F26-D2L-Chapter 2- Introduction to Engineering Materials-Part 1 and part 2 (full).pdf, slide 14
- **Uranium — accuracy note:** the published standard atomic weight of natural uranium is 238.02891 amu. The error in the calculation above comes from the slight difference between the integer-value mass numbers and the actual isotope masses: 238.051 for ²³⁸U, 235.044 for ²³⁵U, and 234.041 for ²³⁴U. All uranium isotopes are radioactive; U-234 has a half-life of 245,500 years and U-238 4.468 billion years, and the ratio of these half-lives (0.0000548) is close to their abundance ratio.
  > Source: EXAMPLE PROBLEMS 1 - WEEK 2.docx, Problem 1
- **Average atomic weight of chromium:** naturally occurring chromium consists of four isotopes — ⁵⁰Cr (4.34% abundance, 49.9460 amu), ⁵²Cr (83.79%, 51.9405 amu), ⁵³Cr (9.50%, 52.9407 amu), and ⁵⁴Cr (2.37%, 53.9389 amu). Ā_Cr = (0.0434)(49.9460 amu) + (0.8379)(51.9405 amu) + (0.0950)(52.9407 amu) + (0.0237)(53.9389 amu) = 51.9963 amu.
  > Source: F26-D2L-Chapter 2- Introduction to Engineering Materials-Part 1 and part 2 (full).pdf, slides 15–16
- **Example Problem 4 — grams per amu and atoms per pound-mole:** (a) 1 g = 6.022 × 10²³ amu, so 1 amu = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g. (b) Since there are 453.6 g/lb_m, a pound-mole contains 2.73 × 10²⁶ atoms/lb-mol.
  > Source: F26-D2L-Chapter 2- Introduction to Engineering Materials-Part 1 and part 2 (full).pdf, slides 7–8, 10; EXAMPLE PROBLEMS 1 - WEEK 2.docx, Problem 4
- **Carbon-12 composition reasoning:** carbon has atomic number 6 regardless of isotope, meaning 6 electrons and — because an atom is electrically neutral — 6 protons; therefore ¹²C has 6 protons and 6 neutrons.
  > Source: EXAMPLE PROBLEMS 1 - WEEK 2.docx, Problem 4

## Common Misconceptions
- Mass and weight are not the same quantity: mass (kg) is the amount of matter and stays the same, while weight (N) is the gravitational force and changes with gravitational acceleration.
  > Source: F26-D2L-Chapter 2- Introduction to Engineering Materials-Part 1 and part 2 (full).pdf, slide 6
- Atomic mass and atomic weight are not interchangeable: atomic mass refers to an individual atom, while atomic weight is the weighted average over naturally occurring isotopes.
  > Source: F26-D2L-Chapter 2- Introduction to Engineering Materials-Part 1 and part 2 (full).pdf, slide 13

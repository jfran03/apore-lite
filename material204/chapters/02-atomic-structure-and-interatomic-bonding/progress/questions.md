## Q001
**Status:** active
**Type:** short-answer
**Difficulty:** introductory
**Topic:** atomic-structure
**Focus Area:** atomic number and atomic mass unit
**Question:** Define atomic number and atomic mass unit (amu).
**Answer:** Atomic number is the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom, which equals the number of electrons in a neutral species. The atomic mass unit (amu) is defined as 1/12 the mass of ¹²C. (See [Atomic Structure](../wiki/atomic-structure.md).)

## Q003
**Status:** active
**Type:** conceptual
**Difficulty:** intermediate
**Topic:** atomic-structure
**Focus Area:** valence electrons
**Question:** What are valence electrons, and why are filled electron shells considered more stable?
**Answer:** Valence electrons are those in the outer unfilled shells. Filled shells are more stable and require more energy to gain or lose electrons; valence electrons are available for bonding and tend to determine an atom's chemical properties. (See [Atomic Structure](../wiki/atomic-structure.md).)

## Q007
**Status:** active
**Type:** short-answer
**Difficulty:** intermediate
**Topic:** bonding-forces-and-energies
**Focus Area:** net energy equation
**Question:** Write the net energy equation for two bonding atoms and explain what each term represents.
**Answer:** E_N = E_A + E_R = -A/r + B/rⁿ, where E_A is attractive energy, E_R is repulsive energy, r is interatomic separation, A is a constant based on the ion charges, and B and n are constants (n ≈ 8). (See [Bonding Forces and Energies](../wiki/bonding-forces-and-energies.md).)

## Q008
**Status:** active
**Type:** conceptual
**Difficulty:** intermediate
**Topic:** bonding-forces-and-energies
**Focus Area:** equilibrium separation
**Question:** What happens to the net energy between two atoms at separations less than the equilibrium separation r₀, and at separations greater than r₀?
**Answer:** At separations less than r₀, repulsive energy dominates the net energy; at separations greater than r₀, attractive energy dominates. The net energy reaches its minimum (most stable point, E₀) at r₀. (See [Bonding Forces and Energies](../wiki/bonding-forces-and-energies.md).)

## Q010
**Status:** active
**Type:** conceptual
**Difficulty:** intermediate
**Topic:** interatomic-bonding-overview
**Focus Area:** directional vs non-directional bonding
**Question:** Explain the difference between directional and non-directional bonding, and which bond types belong to each category.
**Answer:** Directional bonding (covalent bonding and some secondary bonding) involves electrons localized in a specific area between atoms, existing because of the location of electrons or a dipole moment. Non-directional bonding (ionic and metallic bonding) involves electrons that cannot be localized in a specific location, so bonding forces are the same in all directions around the atom. (See [Interatomic Bonding Overview](../wiki/interatomic-bonding-overview.md).)

## Q014
**Status:** active
**Type:** short-answer
**Difficulty:** intermediate
**Topic:** ionic-bonding
**Focus Area:** MgO ionization example
**Question:** In the formation of MgO, describe what happens to the electron configurations of Mg and O.
**Answer:** Mg starts as 1s²2s²2p⁶3s² ([Ne]3s²) and donates its 3s² electrons, becoming Mg²⁺ with configuration 1s²2s²2p⁶ ([Ne]). O starts as 1s²2s²2p⁴ and accepts those electrons, becoming O²⁻ with configuration 1s²2s²2p⁶ ([Ne]). The resulting cation and anion are held together by Coulombic attraction. (See [Ionic Bonding](../wiki/ionic-bonding.md).)

## Q018

**Focus Area:** CH4 bonding example
**Question:** In CH₄, why do carbon and hydrogen share electrons rather than transfer them?
**Answer:** Because the electronegativities of C and H are similar, electrons are shared rather than transferred, forming sp³ hybrid covalent bonds — each carbon needs 4 more electrons (has 4 valence electrons) and each hydrogen needs 1 more (has 1 valence electron), and they overlap orbitals to share electrons. (See [Covalent Bonding](../wiki/covalent-bonding.md).)

## Q021
**Status:** active
**Type:** conceptual
**Difficulty:** intermediate
**Topic:** secondary-bonding
**Focus Area:** fluctuating vs permanent dipoles
**Question:** Distinguish between secondary bonding due to fluctuating dipoles and secondary bonding due to permanent dipoles, giving an example of each.
**Answer:** Fluctuating dipoles arise from asymmetric electron clouds around atoms/molecules (example: liquid H₂, where secondary bonding forms between adjacent H₂ molecules). Permanent dipoles occur in molecules with a fixed + end and - end that bond to other such molecules via secondary bonding (example: liquid HCl). (See [Secondary Bonding](../wiki/secondary-bonding.md).)

**Question:** What makes hydrogen bonding a "special type" of secondary bonding, and how does it affect melting/boiling temperatures?
**Answer:** Hydrogen bonding is a special type of secondary bonding where hydrogen is one of the constituents (examples: HF, H₂O, NH₃). **Question:** According to VSEPR theory, valence electron pairs surrounding an atom: (A) attract each other  (B) repel each other  (C) have no interaction  (D) always form a linear shape
**Answer:** (B) repel each other. VSEPR theory holds that valence electron pairs surrounding an atom tend to repel each other; greater repulsion means higher energy and less stability, and VSEPR predicts the geometry that minimizes this repulsion. ## Q026
**Status:** active
**Type:** conceptual
**Difficulty:** advanced
**Topic:** vsepr-theory-and-molecular-geometry
**Focus Area:** repulsion strength ranking
**Question:** Rank the repulsion strength between lone pairs and bonding pairs from greatest to weakest, and explain how this affects molecular shape distortion.
**Answer:** Repulsion strength from greatest to weakest is: lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bonding pair > bonding pair–bonding pair. Since lone pairs occupy more space than bonding pairs (and the order of sizes from largest to smallest is lone pair > triple bond > double bond > single bond), their stronger repulsion distorts ideal bond angles, pushing bonding pairs closer together. (See [VSEPR Theory and Molecular Geometry](../wiki/vsepr-theory-and-molecular-geometry.md).)

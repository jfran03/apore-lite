# Resistors and Ohm's Law

## Definition
For a resistor, the voltage across the resistor and the current through the resistor are related by Ohm's Law.
> Source: Week 3 - Full.pdf

## Key Concepts
- **The two quantities related:** In Ohm's Law, the voltage is the voltage *across* the resistor and the current is the current *through* the resistor.
  > Source: Week 3 - Full.pdf
- **Case i — current in the direction of voltage drop:** If the current through the resistor is shown in the direction of voltage drop (current enters the + terminal, labelled `i →  + V −` across R), then use **V = iR**. In this case the resistor "looks like an energy-absorbing element."
  > Source: Week 3 - Full.pdf
- **Case ii — current in the direction of voltage rise:** If the current is shown in the direction of voltage rise (current enters the − terminal, labelled `i →  − V +` across R), then use **V = −iR**.
  > Source: Week 3 - Full.pdf
- **Conductance:** From Ohm's Law, i = (1/R)·V. The quantity 1/R is the conductance, G.
  > Source: Week 3 - Full.pdf
- **Unit of conductance:** Siemens (Ω⁻¹), once called the mho (℧).
  > Source: Week 3 - Full.pdf

## Examples
- In the 100 V source / 10 Ω resistor circuit, once V_R = 100 V is established, Ohm's Law gives i_R = V_R / 10 Ω = 100 V / 10 Ω = 10 A.
  > Source: Week 3 - Full.pdf
- In that same circuit, if i_R is instead chosen in the opposite direction (so the current sees the − terminal first), Ohm's Law must be written V_R = −i_R·R, giving i_R = −V_R/R = −100 V / 10 Ω = −10 A — the same *actual* current as before.
  > Source: Week 3 - Full.pdf
